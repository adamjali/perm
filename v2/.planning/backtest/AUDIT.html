Backtest audit: what survives, and what I got wrong

A rebuilt backtest with nested rolling-origin selection, measured censoring, and a queue-reconstruction sensitivity test. The headline: the simplest model wins, almost everything I proposed earlier does not survive, and your 90-day range cap turns out to be achievable honestly.

The finding, up front

A flat 365-day trailing rate, with a bias correction and band that both vary by
predicted horizon, fitted only on earlier forecast origins.

  median absolute error   19 days
  80% band width          95 days   (second pass; the 77d below was optimistic)
  within +/-45 days       78-89%    depending on horizon

REVISED BY A SECOND AUDIT -- see the last section. The first pass missed
47,061 cases and reported a 77-day band. The corrected figure is 95 days.

No blend, no horizon-switched windows, no seasonal index. The thing that carries almost all the value is correcting the bias, not choosing the rate.

1. Origins and maturity

34 twice-monthly origins, 1 Oct 2024 to 15 Feb 2026. An origin is mature only if under 25% of its queue is still unresolved today and it has at least 365 days of rate history behind it.

censoring, measured directly as "share of the T-queue still pending today":

  2024-10-01   0.2%      2025-09-01   2.7%      2025-12-15   16.6%
  2025-03-01   0.8%      2025-11-15   6.2%      2026-01-01   22.1%
  2025-06-01   1.4%      2025-12-01  10.7%      2026-01-15  26.0% IMMATURE
                                                2026-02-15  35.6% IMMATURE

31 of 34 origins are mature. The other three are reported and never scored.

Why this matters. Slow cases are exactly the ones still unresolved, so scoring a recent origin quietly drops its hardest cases and flatters every model. That is the bias the old analysis had and never measured.

2. Queue reconstruction, and its one honest weakness

blocking(T, x) = |{decided : received < x AND decision > T}|   unresolved at T, later resolved
               + |{pending today : filing < x}|              unresolved at T, still unresolved

The second term is the correction the old backtest lacked entirely. The decided-only file cannot see a case that is still open, so every historical queue was undercounted, which made every prediction too early.

The weakness, stated plainly. I cannot reconstruct status history. In production we count only ANALYST REVIEW as blocking, because appeals and holds are out of filing order. Historically there are no status snapshots, so the backtest counts every unresolved case. I ran the whole comparison at three assumptions — 100%, 93.93% (today’s measured analyst share) and 85% — and the winner did not change. Before bias correction the rankings did move; after it they do not, because a constant scale factor is precisely what a bias correction absorbs.

The blend does not earn its complexity. Your idea was conceptually better than my horizon switch — and it is — but it loses to a plain 365-day average on every measure that matters. There is no sharp optimum. 240, 270 and 365 all sit within 11 days of one another and split the wins between them. The correct reading is “somewhere around a year is robust”, not “365 is uniquely right” — and 365 is chosen because it is the simplest point in that plateau and it is what the bias correction was calibrated against, not because it beat 270 by a handful of days.

“June 1.27 / July 0.78” was mostly the shape of 2024, not a season. The monthly index is deleted. Only December and January survive, and those are plainly holidays consuming working days. They are not reinstated as a multiplier until they independently improve held-out origins, which has not been tested yet.

Coverage by predicted horizon, everything fitted on prior origins:

An 80% band is 60–88 days wide at every horizon. So the range you wanted — never more than about December-to-February — is not a compromise. It is what an honest 80% interval actually measures, once the bias is removed. Narrower near the front (62 days inside two months) and wider in the middle, exactly the shape you asked for.

The bias is the whole story: it runs from −1 day to +112 days. Uncorrected, this model is catastrophically late for distant cases. Corrected, it is the best thing tested.

8. Where it fails, and I am not hiding it

  test origin   median error   coverage of its own 80% band
  2025-06-01         18d                   80%
  2025-07-01         22d                   73%
  2025-08-01         36d                   50%
  2025-09-15         56d                   27%
  2025-10-01         53d                   39%
  2025-11-01         13d                   95%
  2025-12-01          8d                   96%

Coverage collapses for forecasts issued in August and September 2025, because they were walking their queues straight into the October 2025 OFLC shutdown. That delay stays in the test error on purpose — it is real forecast risk, and deleting it would be exactly the flattering assumption this audit exists to remove.

So the honest claim is not “80% always”. It is: 80% in the median origin, 90%+ in calm periods, and about 30–50% across an unforecastable federal shock. A disclosure line should say so.

SECOND AUDIT: the first pass was still wrong, and here is what changed

Two challenges were put to the first report: that the huge bias correction might be learning historical regime change rather than a stable property, and that the band was not the monotonic shape it claimed — it got narrower past eleven months. Both were right to ask. Chasing the second one found a much bigger problem.

The 47,061 cases nobody could see

My censoring measure counted cases still pending today. It did not count the third population: cases that are final today but were decided after the disclosure file ends. They appear in neither source the backtest read.

filed 2025-06  5,782      filed 2025-09  12,456
filed 2025-07  9,099      filed 2025-11   6,791
filed 2025-08  8,951      ...

TOTAL 47,061 -- 20.2% of the relevant filing population.

These are slow cases, concentrated in exactly the filing months a long-horizon forecast is about. Leaving them out did two things at once: it undercounted every historical queue, and it deleted the slow tail from the truth set. That is what made far-horizon bands look impossibly narrow. Both are now fixed: they are added to blocking, and scored as right-censored observations at their lower bound, which is the most favourable assumption available.

Correcting it changed the answer. The band is 95 days, not 77. And the window question stopped being a plateau.

1. Which window — no longer a plateau

Every window now gets its own nested bias and range calibration, scored with forecast origins as the resampling unit:

ComparisonMedian paired diffWins
flat 365 vs flat 270−7d (se 1.4)17/19
flat 365 vs flat 240−11d (se 1.8)18/19
flat 450 vs flat 365+5d (se 2.0)7/19
flat 540 vs flat 365−5d (se 3.9)13/18
flat 730 vs flat 365+8d (se 3.7)1/6

365 beats 270 and 240 by five to six standard errors. The one-standard-error rule does not rescue the shorter windows here — they are genuinely worse. Going longer does not help either: 450 is worse, 540 is indistinguishable (−5 ± 3.9), 730 is worse on thin data. The non-monotonic pattern across 365/450/540 is the signature of noise, so 365 is chosen as the shortest of the indistinguishable long windows.

2. Is the bias correction stable? Yes in calm, no across a shock

The far-horizon correction barely moves as the training cutoff advances:

  cutoff        0-2mo  2-4mo  4-7mo  7-11mo  11-16mo  16mo+
  <2025-03-15      -2     11     32     65      86     108
  <2025-07-15       2     20     37     51      81     111
  <2025-11-15      -1      7     16     39      81     111
  <2026-01-01      -1      7     19     44      83     111

The +111-day correction is stable to about three days. The mid-range is the unstable part (4–7 months swings 32 → 16). So the worry was reasonable and the answer is mostly reassuring — with one caveat: nested stability inside a single era is not proof it survives a regime change, and every origin here is from one era.

But the correction is a fair-weather instrument. It helps in 13 of 19 held-out origins and hurts in 6 — and the 6 are all the origins forecasting into the October 2025 shutdown, where it turns 23d of error into 42d. It makes a good model better in calm periods and a bad situation worse in a shock.

3. The monotonic band: 95 days, not 90

A genuine 80% interval peaks at 95 days. So the 90-day product cap is five days short of honest, not the comfortable fit the first pass claimed. Two honest options: publish 95 days and call it 80%, or cap at 90 and label the real coverage, which is about 78% at the widest point. I would publish 95 and keep the round number out of it.

The residual narrowing at 11–16 and 16+ months is smaller than before but has not gone away, and those bins draw on only 17–19 independent origins against 31 for the near bins. The monotone constraint holds the width at 95 rather than letting it fall, which is the right treatment for a shape we cannot yet justify.

4. Holidays: real, tiny, and worth keeping anyway

  ordinary weekday    522/day
  federal holiday      10/day    ratio 0.02
  Dec 24 - Jan 1      113/day    ratio 0.22

paired improvement -1 day (se 0.22), wins 13/19 origins

Measured as future calendar dates, never as a monthly multiplier, and normalised to mean 1 over a year so it redistributes rather than adds. Statistically real (four standard errors) and practically worth one day. I would keep it, mostly because a queue simulation that spends decisions on Christmas Day is wrong on its face, and the list is static and free.

What remains uncertain

  • One era. All 31 origins sit inside a single stretch of DOL history. Nested stability is not the same as surviving a regime change.
  • Long horizons rest on 17–19 origins, and their censored members are scored at a lower bound, so their errors are still optimistic by an unknown amount.
  • Shocks break everything, the bias correction worst of all. Coverage falls to 27–50% across the shutdown.
  • Status history does not exist, so the backtest counts every unresolved case as blocking while production counts only analyst review. Sensitivity-tested; the ranking held.

Verdict: cleared to rebuild. Flat 365-day rate, nested horizon-dependent bias correction, monotone 95-day 80% band, explicit holiday calendar. Not because it is elegant, but because it is the only configuration that survived being attacked twice.

What I have NOT done

  • Not shipped anything. No production constant has changed. The estimator is exactly as it was.
  • Not tested a three-timescale blend — two components already failed to beat one, so a third has nothing to earn its place against.
  • Not tested regime detection or damped trend. They are plausible and unmeasured, and the same rule applies: beat a flat 365 on held-out origins or stay out.
  • Not re-tested Dec/Jan holidays as a multiplier. Next candidate, and the only survivor of the seasonal work.
  • Not extended the search past 730 days. The optimum is interior, so the range is wide enough.
