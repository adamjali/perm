This is for HR teams and employers budgeting a PERM, and for workers who've been asked to chip in. The short answer: the employer pays for the labor certification, including its own attorney's fees, and it can't pass those costs to the worker in money, in a paycheck deduction or in unpaid work. That's 20 C.F.R. § 656.12(b) (2025), in force since July 16, 2007.

<Callout type="important" title="The rule, in its own words">
"An employer must not seek or receive payment of any kind for any activity related to obtaining permanent labor certification, including payment of the employer's attorneys' fees, whether as an incentive or inducement to filing, or as a reimbursement for costs incurred in preparing or filing a permanent labor certification application." 20 C.F.R. § 656.12(b)
</Callout>

DOL put it plainly when it adopted the rule: employers file "with the full understanding that the costs they incur for the preparation and filing of the application and obtaining permanent labor certification are to be exclusively borne by the employer" (72 Fed. Reg. at 27,919).

## What counts as payment

The regulation lists it, and the list is wider than a check from the worker. Under § 656.12(b), payment covers these, and the list is open-ended:

- monetary payments
- wage concessions, including deductions from wages, salary or benefits
- kickbacks, bribes or tributes
- in kind payments
- free labor

DOL's 2007 explanation adds that "any attempt by an employer to recover labor certification costs from an employee through deductions from wages, uncompensated additional work by the employee, or otherwise" would be an attempt to get around the rule. An agreement that takes the money back later, after the case is filed or approved, is an attempt to recover it "otherwise". Whether a particular agreement crosses the line is a question for the employer's counsel.

## No exemption by employer type

When DOL proposed the rule, nonprofits, hospitals, public universities and small businesses asked to keep cost-sharing with the worker. DOL declined: "The Department disagrees. The recruitment, legal, and other costs associated with labor certification are transaction costs necessary for or, in the case of legal fees, desired by the employer to complete the labor market test" (72 Fed. Reg. at 27,923). The rule applies to every employer that files.

## What happens if it's broken

Under § 656.12(c), evidence that an employer sought or received payment "shall be grounds for investigation" and may lead to:

<StepByStep>
<Step number={1} title="Denial">
Of the application, under 20 C.F.R. § 656.24.
</Step>
<Step number={2} title="Revocation">
Of a certification already granted, under 20 C.F.R. § 656.32.
</Step>
<Step number={3} title="Debarment">
Of the employer from the program, under 20 C.F.R. § 656.31(f).
</Step>
</StepByStep>

DOL's reason goes to the heart of PERM: "Permanent labor certification is an employer-driven process; employers, not aliens, must file permanent labor certification applications" (72 Fed. Reg. at 27,920). A worker paying the employer's costs, in DOL's words, "casts suspicion on the integrity of the process and the existence of a bona fide job opportunity." *Id.*

## What the rule doesn't decide

- **Later steps.** Section 656.12 is about the labor certification. It doesn't address the I-140 or the I-485; ask the attorney how those are handled.
- **The worker's own lawyer.** A worker may hire and pay a separate attorney. If one attorney represents both sides, the employer pays.
- **Fee amounts.** No government source publishes what PERM legal work costs, and quotes depend on the firm and the case, so there are no ranges here. Nothing on this site is legal advice; the attorney on the case is the person to ask about a specific arrangement.

## For HR: a budget checklist

<PrintableChecklist title="PERM cost checklist for employers">

- [ ] Attorney fees for the prevailing wage request, recruitment and the ETA-9089, on the employer's budget
- [ ] Recruitment advertising (print ads and any additional steps), on the employer's budget
- [ ] No deduction, bonus clawback or repayment clause tied to the labor certification
- [ ] If one firm represents both employer and worker, the employer pays its fees for the labor certification
- [ ] The engagement letter says who the firm represents
- [ ] Records kept for 5 years from filing (20 C.F.R. § 656.10(f))

</PrintableChecklist>
