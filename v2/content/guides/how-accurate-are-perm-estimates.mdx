On one day in August 2026, six public PERM tools were asked the same headline question, how long a PERM takes, and answered between 335 and 503 days. DOL's own published average that day was 372. The spread is five months, far beyond rounding, on a wait that people plan their lives around. This guide is what an estimate can know, what it can't, and what we measured when we checked our own. PERM Tracker's [processing times page](/perm-processing-times) shows DOL's own published figures beside our own daily check of where DOL is actually deciding.

## What an estimate actually is

Every PERM estimator does the same arithmetic underneath. DOL works cases roughly in filing order, so there's a filing month it's deciding now, and your case has a filing month. The estimate is the distance between the two, converted to days using how fast the queue has been moving. Everything else a tool adds, an employer's initial, a "confidence" label, a colour, sits on top of that one subtraction. DOL printed the month it was deciding until October 31, 2026; since then it has to be found from DOL's own case statuses, which this site does every day.

Ours counts instead of subtracting months: the cases in DOL's normal queue filed before yours, divided by the rate DOL has actually been deciding them, walked over the days DOL works. A weekend, a federal holiday or the slow days around Thanksgiving and the new year move a date the way they move DOL. Cases on hold, at a request for information or on appeal are out of line, so they aren't counted as ahead of you.

That's also why estimates fail the same way. When DOL's queue slows, every tool built on the previous pace is late together. When DOL reorders work, as it did after the October 2025 shutdown, every tool is wrong in the same direction at once.

## What we measured

Every night the site rebuilds DOL's line as it stood a few weeks earlier, works out the date it would have given each case then, and sets each one against the day DOL actually decided it. It does the same for every other kind of date it gives: a case DOL's line has already passed, a case at a request for information, a wage request, a priority date waiting on the visa bulletin, and H-2A, H-2B and CW-1 applications. The second number is the one every rival talks about and few publish: how much the employer's initial moves a decision. Across DOL's decided cases, a company starting with A has tended to be reached before one starting with Z. Its size is the question, so we measured it over 339,518 decided cases.

Historically the effect was 27 days end to end: A about 11 days earlier than the average case in its month, Z about 16 days later. The published claim of plus or minus 80 days is the same effect inflated roughly six times. In 6 of the 30 filing months we could check, the order ran backwards. And at the front of the queue in September 2026 it didn't show at all: DOL decided about 80% of each letter's cases in the same fortnight. Adding it to our dates made them worse, so they don't use it.

## What each estimator uses

Any estimator can know DOL's queue position and how fast the queue has moved. None can know about an audit, an RFI or a hold before DOL issues it.

## When we refuse to give a date

We print no date for a case on appeal, for an RFI that's run past its window, or for a passed filing month when the rate DOL finishes those cases can't be read.

<Callout type="important" title="If DOL has already passed your filing month">
The filing-month arithmetic has nothing left to say: its date has gone by. A case page then dates the case by the rate DOL has actually been finishing the cases its line left behind, which the check above holds to account. The processing time calculator, which knows only a month and not a case, gives no date; it says how that month's cases are splitting and the rate they're finishing at. A case still pending well behind the line is often in an audit, a request for information or a hold, and the case lookup shows the status DOL actually holds.
</Callout>

An appeal is a separate proceeding, so its page shows how long such cases have waited and gives no date. An RFI is dated from its own RFI day, through the 30 days the employer has to answer and the days DOL usually takes after; once it has run past both, it usually has an extension or a second request, and neither can be dated. A blank is a true answer, and a confident wrong date isn't one.

## What this can't tell you

This guide can't tell you your decision date, whether your case will be audited, anything about a case DOL hasn't received, or how another tool got its number.

- Your decision date. Near the front of the queue our dates have typically landed within a few days; further out there's more room to miss, and the [estimate scorecard](/estimate-scorecard) shows how much.
- Whether your case will be audited. Nothing can, until DOL issues it.
- Anything about a case DOL hasn't received. An estimate needs a filing month, which needs a filing.
- How another tool got its number. Where a rival publishes a method we describe it; where it doesn't, we say so on the [methodology page](/methodology).
