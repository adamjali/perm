This guide is for the attorney or HR lead at a sponsor that has laid people off, or plans to, while PERM cases are in preparation. The rule doesn't bar a filing. It adds a duty: notify and consider the laid-off workers for the job, and be able to show it.

<Callout type="important" title="The rule, 20 CFR 656.17(k)(1)">
"If there has been a layoff by the employer applicant in the area of intended employment within 6 months of filing an application involving the occupation for which certification is sought or in a related occupation, the employer must document it has notified and considered all potentially qualified laid off (employer applicant) U.S. workers of the job opportunity involved in the application and the results of the notification and consideration. A layoff shall be considered any involuntary separation of one or more employees without cause or prejudice."
</Callout>

## Four questions decide whether it applies

<ComparisonTable
  headers={["Question", "What the rule says", "Citation"]}
  rows={[
    ["Was it a layoff?", "Any involuntary separation of one or more employees without cause or prejudice. One person counts. A dismissal for cause doesn't", "20 CFR 656.17(k)(1)"],
    ["In the right place?", "In the area of intended employment: within normal commuting distance of the worksite, and anywhere in the same metropolitan statistical area", "656.17(k)(1), 656.3"],
    ["In the right occupation?", "The occupation being certified, or a related one: any occupation \"that requires workers to perform a majority of the essential duties\" of it", "656.17(k)(1) and (2)"],
    ["In the right window?", "Within the 6 months before the application is filed", "656.17(k)(1)"]
  ]}
/>

All four have to be true for the rule to reach the case. A layoff of accountants doesn't touch a software engineer's PERM unless the jobs share most of their essential duties, and a layoff in another city outside the commuting area doesn't touch it either.

## The six months run back from filing

The window is measured from the filing date, which at the start of a case is usually months away. So a layoff that's outside the window today can be inside it by the time the ETA-9089 goes in.

- Filing on Tuesday, December 1, 2026 looks back to Monday, June 1, 2026.
- A layoff on Tuesday, September 15, 2026 stays inside the window for any filing through Monday, March 15, 2027.

That's why intake asks about planned layoffs as well as past ones. A cut announced after recruitment but before filing still lands in the six months.

## What the employer has to do

<StepByStep>
<Step number={1} title="Identify the laid-off workers the rule reaches">
U.S. workers the employer laid off, in the area, in the occupation or a related one, within the window. The rule asks for "all potentially qualified" ones.
</Step>
<Step number={2} title="Notify them of the job">
Tell each of them about the specific job opportunity in the application. Keep the notice, its date, and how it was sent.
</Step>
<Step number={3} title="Consider each of them">
Review each one against the job's stated requirements, as with any applicant, with the lawful job-related reason recorded for anyone not hired.
</Step>
<Step number={4} title="Document the results">
The rule asks for "the results of the notification and consideration." The recruitment report the employer prepares under 656.17(g)(1) is the natural place to state them.
</Step>
</StepByStep>

## How the ETA-9089 records it

Question G.12 asks: "Has the employer had a layoff in the occupation involved in this application or in a related occupation within the 6 months immediately preceding the filing of this application in the area of intended employment?" DOL's general instructions say a Yes needs an Appendix C entry to "explain briefly the nature of the layoff specifying the job title(s), job location(s), and the number of employees laid off," and how the laid-off employees "were notified of the job opportunity listed in this application."

## What to keep in the file

- The list of separations in the area for the six months before filing, with job titles, dates and whether each was involuntary
- The comparison of each laid-off job's duties against the PERM job, for the related-occupation question
- Each notice sent to a laid-off worker, with its date and method
- Responses, résumés and the reason for each decision
- The Appendix C text as filed

This is supporting documentation, kept for five years from filing under 656.10(f). The [document checklist](/guides/perm-document-checklist) lists what else the file keeps.

## What the record shows

As of October 16, 2026, the [denial rates page](/perm-denial-risk) counts 10,222 decided cases in DOL's published files for fiscal years 2024 to 2026 (through the third quarter of 2026) where the employer answered Yes to the layoff question. 56 of them were denied, 0.55%, against 3.20% for all decided cases. That's an association. It says nothing about why those cases were decided as they were.

The [layoff notices page](/layoffs) lists WARN Act notices from California, Texas, New York and Washington, matched by name to PERM employers. A WARN notice and a 656.17(k) layoff are different things: WARN covers large layoffs and closings under labor law, while 656.17(k) reaches one involuntary separation. A cut too small for a WARN notice can still be inside the PERM rule, and a WARN notice at another site or in another occupation can be outside it.

## A change that's planned and not yet proposed

DOL's Fall 2025 regulatory agenda lists a planned PERM proposal (RIN 1205-AC29) whose summary includes "strengthening safeguards for U.S. Workers impacted by layoffs." As of October 16, 2026 the Federal Register holds no document under that RIN, so the rule above is the rule. The [policy changes page](/policy-changes) lists Federal Register documents as they appear.

## What this guide can't decide

- Whether two jobs share a majority of essential duties, or whether a worksite is within commuting distance of a layoff. Those are judgments on the facts for the attorney on the case. Nothing here is legal advice.
- Whether a separation was for cause. That's the employer's record to make and keep.
