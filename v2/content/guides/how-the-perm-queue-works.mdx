Every PERM application waits in the same line, and the line is ordered by the month the application was filed. That is the whole model. Everything else is detail sitting on top of it.

This walks through what "roughly first in, first out" means in practice, what the one number the Department of Labor publishes actually says, and where the model stops being useful.

## Who gets decided first?

DOL's analyst review queue works in filing order. An application filed in October 2025 is looked at before one filed in December 2025, whatever the employer, the job, the state, or how urgent it is for the person waiting.

"Analyst review" is the status DOL's case system gives a PERM application while it waits in that line, and most applications carry it from filing until a decision. It doesn't mean anything is wrong with the case. How long it lasts depends on how far your filing month is from the one DOL is working: <DolNow show="queue" />

It's filing month order, not filing day order. DOL clears a month over several weeks. Inside the month it has tended to go roughly alphabetically by employer name, though not reliably (more on that below).

Not every case rides that queue the whole way. On our daily sweep in late September 2026, the cases DOL hadn't decided yet split like this:

| Where a waiting case sits | Cases | What that means |
|---|---|---|
| Analyst review | 90,127 | the ordinary queue, worked in filing order |
| Out of filing order | 3,074 | an audit, an information request, supervised recruitment or a hold |
| Under appeal or review | 2,721 | went to appeal after an adverse determination |

Only the first group moves in filing order. That's the honest answer to "DOL has passed my month and I still have nothing": a case in the second or third group has left the line, and its filing month stopped predicting anything the day it did.

## What is the published frontier?

Once a week DOL publishes one sentence about the queue: the filing month its analysts are currently reviewing. On 28 August 2026 that was November 2025. Audited cases have their own, slower position, which sat at December 2025.

That is the frontier. It's the front of the line, and it's the single most useful public fact about where a case stands. Subtract your filing month from it and you have your distance in queue terms.

Two things about it catch people out.

DOL overwrites the figure every time it publishes. Its own page can never say how fast the queue is moving, because it only ever shows today's position. Keeping every reading is the only way to see the steps, which is what [processing times](/perm-processing-times) does.

The second thing is that the frontier is DOL's own claim about itself, not a measurement of the pile. Scanning cases one by one puts the oldest month that isn't substantially decided at October 2025, a month behind the November 2025 DOL published. Both figures are shown as they are, with neither adjusted towards the other. They measure slightly different things, and neither one is wrong.

## Does the employer's first letter matter?

Less than you'll read elsewhere, and right now not at all.

Across the decided record, DOL has tended to work a filing month alphabetically by employer name, so an A company's case was usually decided before a Z company's from the same month. Every public estimator uses this. Almost none of them says how large the effect is, or checks whether it still holds.

Measured against the average across the decided corpus:

| | Compared to the average |
|---|---|
| A, the fastest letter | 11.4 days earlier |
| Z, the slowest | 15.7 days later |
| The alphabet end to end | about 27 days |

It also isn't reliable. In 6 of 30 filing months the back half of the alphabet came out faster than the front.

And at the front of the queue it has stopped showing. We rebuilt the queue as it stood on 13 September 2026 and followed what DOL decided over the next two weeks: about 80% of November 2025's normal-queue cases were decided whatever the employer's initial, U and Z included. Adding the letter made our estimates worse (a typical miss of 3.9 days without it, 6.5 with it), so they no longer use it. Your filing month is what matters.

## Why isn't a queue position a promise?

Because the queue's speed isn't stable.

Over the six months of determinations to June 2026, DOL cleared 1.8 months of filing queue for every calendar month that passed. Anything above 1.0 means it's gaining on the backlog. Across the whole record on file, that rate has run between 0.33 and 2.50. A forecast built on the fast years and one built on the slow years land months apart, and both used real DOL data.

The arithmetic people reach for first doesn't work either. DOL issued 19,787 determinations in June 2026, against 95,922 cases still waiting. Dividing one into the other gives about five months and means little: it counts cases filed after yours, and cases on hold or at an RFI that aren't in the line at all.

What a date here does instead is count the cases in DOL's normal queue filed before yours and divide by the pace DOL has actually been deciding them over the last four weeks. Every date is graded against real decisions on the [estimate scorecard](/estimate-scorecard). A single date quoted without a range and a record behind it is a guess with the error bars taken off.

## What this can't tell you

- **When a specific case will be decided.** A filing month gives you an order, not a date.
- **Which of the three groups a case is in**, unless DOL's own status says so. That comes from the case number, not from the queue.
- **Why DOL prioritises the way it does.** The order inside a month is observable. The reasoning behind a reprioritisation isn't published.
- **Anything about the last few hours.** These figures are refreshed daily from DOL. A case decided this morning shows on DOL's system first.
- **Whether the current pace holds.** Every rate on this page describes what already happened. None of them is a commitment by anyone.
