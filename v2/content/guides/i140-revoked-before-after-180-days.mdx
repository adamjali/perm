This guide is for anyone who has left an employer, or been laid off, with an approved I-140 behind them. The question is usually "can they take it away?" The answer turns on one date: how long the I-140 had been approved when the employer withdrew it.

<Callout type="important" title="The short answer">
Withdrawn less than 180 days after approval: automatically revoked, unless an I-485 based on it has been pending 180 days. Withdrawn 180 days or more after approval: it "remains approved unless its approval is revoked on other grounds" (8 CFR 205.1(a)(3)(iii)(C)). Either way, the old employer's job offer is gone. After 180 days, what survives is the approval and its priority date.
</Callout>

## The timing, case by case

A withdrawal or the employer going out of business revokes an approved I-140 only within its first 180 days, unless an I-485 has been pending 180 days. An invalidated labor certification revokes it at any time.

**A worked date.** An I-140 approved on Monday, April 20, 2026 reaches 180 days on Saturday, October 17, 2026. A withdrawal filed on October 16 is "less than 180 days after approval" and revokes it. One filed on October 17 or later leaves it approved.

## What the employer can still take away

A withdrawal after 180 days rescinds the job offer. The regulation says that when a petition is withdrawn, "the job offer of the petitioning employer is rescinded and the alien must obtain a new employment-based preference petition in order to seek adjustment of status or issuance of an immigrant visa". So the surviving approval keeps your place in line. It doesn't, by itself, get you a green card.

The exception is portability. If your I-485 has been pending 180 days, INA 204(j) keeps the petition valid for a new job "in the same or a similar occupational classification", filed on Supplement J (8 CFR 245.25).

## The priority date: kept, with four exceptions

An approved petition gives you its priority date "for any subsequently filed petition for any classification under section 203(b)(1), (2), or (3)" (8 CFR 204.5(e)(1)). A new employer's PERM and I-140 can carry it. The date is lost only if USCIS revokes the approval because of one of four things (204.5(e)(2)):

1. Fraud, or a willful misrepresentation of a material fact
2. DOL revoking the labor certification behind it
3. USCIS or the State Department invalidating that labor certification
4. A USCIS determination that the approval rested on a material error

An employer's withdrawal isn't on that list. The [priority date retention calculator](/tools/priority-date-retention) walks your own dates through the rules.

## Revocation on other grounds

Separately from withdrawal, USCIS can revoke an approval "upon notice to the petitioner on any ground other than those specified in § 205.1" (8 CFR 205.2). That takes a notice of intent, a chance to respond with evidence, and a written decision. The petitioner has 15 days after the notice of revocation to appeal. The notice goes to the petitioner, which is the employer, so a former employee may not hear of it directly. Ask the attorney who represents you now to keep watch.

## What it means for your H-1B and your family

An approval that stands still supports three-year H-1B extensions past six years, even with a new employer, and an H-4 spouse's work permit.

- **Three-year H-1B extensions past six years** need "the beneficiary of an approved immigrant visa petition" whose date is held back only by the per-country limit, and they run until a final decision to revoke the approval (8 CFR 214.2(h)(13)(iii)(E)). A new H-1B employer can use the old approval ((E)(4)).
- **An H-4 spouse's work permit** needs the H-1B to be the beneficiary of an approved I-140, or to hold H-1B time past six years under AC21 (8 CFR 214.2(h)(9)(iv)).

The [six-year guide](/guides/h1b-six-year-limit-with-perm-pending) covers the extensions with dates.

## What to keep, and what to ask

Keep your own copies of the approval notice, the certified ETA 9089 and the receipt numbers, write down three dates, and ask the new employer's attorney three questions.

<StepByStep>
  <Step number={1} title="Keep your own copies">
    The I-140 approval notice, the certified ETA 9089 and the receipt numbers. A new employer's attorney will want them, and it's easier to keep them now than to ask a former employer later.
  </Step>
  <Step number={2} title="Write down three dates">
    The I-140 approval date, the I-485 receipt date if you have one, and the date you left.
  </Step>
  <Step number={3} title="Ask the new employer's attorney">
    Can the new I-140 carry my old priority date? Is my I-485 past 180 days, so that Supplement J applies? Can the old approval support my next H-1B extension?
  </Step>
</StepByStep>

Nothing here is legal advice. The rule turns on dates and documents the attorney can check.

## What this guide can't tell you

This guide can't tell you whether your former employer withdrew the I-140, whether a new job is "same or similar", or what a new PERM will need.

- **Whether your former employer withdrew the I-140.** The withdrawal goes to USCIS, and neither DOL's record nor this site shows it.
- **Whether a new job is "same or similar".** USCIS decides that on Supplement J.
- **What a new PERM will need.** That's a new case at a new employer, with its own recruitment and wait.
