This guide is for someone told by their employer or its attorney that "the labor market test failed" or "recruitment wasn't successful". It's a hard thing to hear with an H-1B clock running. Here's what it means, what it doesn't, and how to work out how much time you have.

<Callout type="info" title="The short answer">
A qualified U.S. worker answered the job ads, so the employer can't truthfully file the PERM from that recruitment. Nothing went to DOL, so there's no case, no status to look up and no priority date. The employer can recruit again later. What matters for your H-1B is how soon a PERM can be filed, because the extensions past six years count from the filing.
</Callout>

## What "failed" means

A PERM rests on the employer's attestations. Two of them, from [20 CFR 656.10(c)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-20/chapter-V/part-656/subpart-C/section-656.10), are the ones a successful applicant tests:

- "The job opportunity has been and is clearly open to any U.S. worker" (656.10(c)(8))
- "The U.S. workers who applied for the job opportunity were rejected for lawful job-related reasons" (656.10(c)(9))

DOL certifies only when there's no U.S. worker "able, willing, qualified, and available" for the job (656.24(b)(2)). And the bar for "qualified" is lower than people expect. Under 656.17(g)(2), a U.S. worker counts as able and qualified if they can learn the missing skills "during a reasonable period of on-the-job training." So an applicant who's close enough can end a recruitment.

The attorney reviews each applicant against those rules. When one qualifies, the employer can't sign those attestations, so the PERM doesn't go in.

## What it does and doesn't do

Failed recruitment files nothing with DOL, so it creates no priority date. The employer can try again, and your H-1B status isn't touched.

Some employers pause for months before trying again. That's company policy. The regulation doesn't require it, so it's fair to ask the attorney whether the pause is the employer's choice or a legal need.

## What a second attempt takes

The steps, in the order the regulation puts them:

<StepByStep>
  <Step number={1} title="A prevailing wage determination">
    The employer asks DOL for the wage. <DolNow show="pwd" />
  </Step>
  <Step number={2} title="The mandatory recruitment">
    For a professional job, a 30-day job order with the state workforce agency and ads on two different Sundays in a newspaper. These "must be conducted at least 30 days, but no more than 180 days, before the filing of the application" (656.17(e)(1)(i)).
  </Step>
  <Step number={3} title="Three more steps from the regulation's list">
    Only one of them "may consist solely of activity that took place within 30 days of the filing" (656.17(e)(1)(ii)).
  </Step>
  <Step number={4} title="The filing">
    The day DOL accepts the PERM is the day both clocks start: your priority date, and the 365 days toward an H-1B extension.
  </Step>
</StepByStep>

The 30-day job order plus the 30 days the mandatory steps must sit before filing put the filing at least about two months after the job order starts, and the wage determination comes before that.

## The H-1B arithmetic

The one-year extensions past six years need a PERM or I-140 filed at least 365 days before the extension would start (8 CFR 214.2(h)(13)(iii)(D)). Failed recruitment produced no filing, so it started no clock.

Work backward from your end date. It's 365 days, so a February 29 inside the year moves the answer by a day, as in the second row:

| Your six years end | A one-year extension would start | A PERM must be filed by |
|---|---|---|
| Thursday, September 30, 2027 | Friday, October 1, 2027 | Thursday, October 1, 2026 |
| Friday, March 31, 2028 | Saturday, April 1, 2028 | Friday, April 2, 2027 |

If that filing date is already close or past, other routes may still exist, and they're the attorney's to weigh:

- **An earlier PERM or I-140 for you that's still alive**, from this employer or a past one. The H-1B employer doesn't have to be the one that filed it ((D)(6)).
- **An approved I-140** with a date held back by the per-country limit, which supports three-year extensions under (E).
- **Recaptured time** spent outside the United States during your H-1B, which moves the end date later (8 CFR 214.2(h)(13)(iii)(C)).
- **A year outside the United States**, after which the six years start over (8 CFR 214.2(h)(13)(iii)(A)).

The [six-year limit calculator](/tools/h1b-six-year-limit) puts your own dates in, and the [six-year guide](/guides/h1b-six-year-limit-with-perm-pending) covers both exemptions.

## Ask the attorney

Ask whether the applicant was found qualified, when recruitment can start again, the latest filing date for your next H-1B extension, and whether an earlier PERM or I-140 still counts.

1. Was the applicant found qualified, or is the employer still deciding?
2. When can recruitment start again, and is any pause a legal need or a company policy?
3. What's the latest date a new PERM can be filed and still support my next H-1B extension?
4. Do I have any earlier PERM or I-140 that still counts?

Nothing here is legal advice. Your dates decide this, and the attorney has the full picture.

## What this guide can't tell you

It can't tell you whether the applicant really was qualified or whether the next recruitment will succeed.

- **Whether the applicant really was qualified.** That's a judgment on the résumé against the job's stated requirements.
- **Whether the next recruitment will succeed.** It depends on who answers.
- **Which employers file PERMs for jobs like yours.** That part the record can show: the [sponsor finder](/sponsor-finder) lists employers by what they've filed.
